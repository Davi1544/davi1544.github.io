# Week 7 - First Steps in ICA

## Overview
Following the progression of the last weeks, I spent Week 7 adding a new step to our processing pipeline: ICA.

In this post, we'll discuss my main results from applying ICA to our dataset and the challenges (or limitations) around it.

## Experiments

### Removing noise: ICA

ICA (Independent Component Analysis) is a technique used to split a signal into independent components. I decided to apply ICA to our dataset following the method used in ["Palimpsest Research Based on Hyperspectral Information"](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10113438). The assumption behind the application of ICA to our dataset is that our images (which we can interpret as signals) are the weighted sum of independent components.

Fortunately, we can comfortably think of our images as the overlap of four well-defined layers: paper, undertext, overtext and noise (e. g., mold or discoloration), even though these components are not actually fully independent. Our goal, then, is for ICA to split our images into these four components, where the undertext component is the one containing the palimpsest.

We'll conduct all experiments on the following image:

![RGB conversion](/assets/img/posts/week7/colorido.png)

To apply ICA to our images, we must first reduce the number of dimensions from 16 to a smaller number using PCA. This step is necessary because ICA becomes computationally expensive on our dataset, especially since every pixel is treated as a sample whose features correspond to the multispectral channels.

By reducing the number of channels from 16 to 3 and then applying ICA, we get the following components:

![ICA 16 to 3 - full image - text component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_full_text.png)

![ICA 16 to 3 - full image - texture component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_full_texture.png)

![ICA 16 to 3 - full image - noise component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_full_noise.png)

Observe that the components are remarkably well-defined. The first isolates the text, the second isolates the noise (mold), and the third isolates the texture of the paper (creases, symbols, etc).

Despite the fact that the overtext and undertext were not separated, this result is already promising, as we can now read the text without the visual noise around it.

If we instead reduce the number of channels from 16 to 4 and then apply ICA, we get the following four components:

![ICA 16 to 4 - full image - text component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_4_full_text.png)

![ICA 16 to 4 - full image - texture component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_4_full_texture.png)

![ICA 16 to 4 - full image - noise component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_4_full_noise.png)

![ICA 16 to 4 - full image - gradient component](/assets/img/posts/week7/ica_4_full_gradient.png)

Observe that the undertext and overtext have not yet been separated and the components are now harder to interpret. So while this experiment took significantly longer than the previous one, it did not yield better results. This suggests that, at least for this sample, ICA alone may not be enough to separate undertext and overtext.

Do keep in mind, though, that the reference article also suggested the need of human experts to fully separate the text. 

### Optimizing ICA

As discussed in the previous section, the large size of the images in our dataset leads to more computing time when applying ICA. This problem creates a restriction to how much information ICA can actually get about the original data before computing time becomes prohibitive.

To improve in this aspect, I wanted to test if applying ICA to local parts of the image would yield good enough results, as this would mean we could allow ICA to more of the original data without running into a computing time problem.

This optimization requires validation because a local chunk of the image might not represent the original image well.

We'll compare the results of this method with the previous one on the same image as before, focusing on the following patch (that contains major influence from all theorical components):

![cropped RGB conversion](/assets/img/posts/week7/colorido_cropped.png)

Our ultimate goal is to check how well the local results compare to the global ones.

In summary, our goal is to test if the much faster pipeline of "Crop to Process" achieves similar results to the original "Process to Crop" pipeline. 

After reducing the number of components on the patch from 16 to 3 and then applying ICA, the results were promising enough that we can easily pair the produced local components to their global cropped versions. In the following series of images, the images at the top come from the global components while the ones at the bottom come from the local components.

On the text component, we achieved the following results:

![compares P2R with R2P - text component (P2R)](/assets/img/posts/week7/p2r/picture_to_recorte_text.png)

![compares P2R with R2P - text component (R2P)](/assets/img/posts/week7/r2p/recorte_to_picture_text.png)

Likewise, the texture component yielded very similar results:

![compares P2R with R2P - texture component (P2R)](/assets/img/posts/week7/p2r/picture_to_recorte_texture.png)

![compares P2R with R2P - texture component (R2P)](/assets/img/posts/week7/r2p/recorte_to_picture_texture.png)

Finally, the noise component:

![compares P2R with R2P - noise component (P2R)](/assets/img/posts/week7/p2r/picture_to_recorte_noise.png)

![compares P2R with R2P - noise component (R2P)](/assets/img/posts/week7/r2p/recorte_to_picture_noise.png)

As we can see, the differences are mostly marginal. In fact, the only noticeable difference lies in the noise component, where the big "1846" in the first image (global ICA application) cannot be seen at all in the other image. Keep in mind, though, that the number can be clearly seen in the texture component in both cases.

These results suggest that while localized ICA produces different results for this image, this method could be safely applied to our dataset instead of global ICA, as the substantial reduction in computing time far outweighs the slight loss in result quality.

Although these results are encouraging, this experiment and its implications bear important limitations, mostly because I chose a very particular patch of the image that contains a substantial contribution from each component, which is probably not the case for the average patch.

Therefore, throughout the next few weeks and experiments, I'll continue to test both local and global ICA to make sure local ICA is not affecting their ultimate conclusions.
