# Week 8 - Exploring CLAHE

## Overview
Following the progression of the previous weeks, I spent Week 8 adding a new step to our processing pipeline: contrast correction. We'll discuss the use of CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization).

In this post, we'll discuss some experiments I've conducted with CLAHE, applying the technique to different parts of the pipeline.

## Experiments

### Artificial Coloring: CLAHE on PCA's false colors

CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) is a contrast enhancement technique that applies histogram equalization locally. In the context of MSI visualization, contrast enhancement serves as a relatively cheap technique for making features in the image more visible.

In our reference article, CLAHE was used in post-processing during the artificial coloring step in two different pipelines, one before and one after ICA.

In this first experiment, we'll evaluate the results of applying CLAHE before ICA (i. e., right after PCA). Our main goal is to test how much CLAHE improves the visualization of artifacts in the image.

Just like in last week's experiment, we'll experiment on the following patch of one of the images in our dataset:

![cropped RGB conversion](/assets/img/posts/week7/colorido_cropped.png)

The application of PCA (from 16 channels to 3) to this patch results in the following image:

![Direct PCA patch](/assets/img/posts/week8/pca_rgb.png)

By further processing this result through CLAHE with a kernel size of 30 pixels, this is the resulting image:

![CLAHE 30 pixels patch](/assets/img/posts/week8/pca_rgb_equalized_30.png)

As we can see, while CLAHE did not make the undertext more visible, it had an interesting effect on the image that I would describe as making different regions of the image more visually defined. By this, I mean that, after CLAHE, most of the gradients in the image are gone. The text has a clear yellow border around it that wasn't as clear in the original image. The numbers on the back became darker, while the surrounding paper became lighter. Furthermore, the dark pixels on the paper, which appeared essentially as a gradient in the original PCA image, are now more clearly defined and resemble the noise component we detected in our last experiment.

Before we conclude this experiment, we must discuss the importance of the kernel size used. In the context of CLAHE, the kernel size determines the size of the local region used to calculate the equalized values. 

If we set the kernel size to 10 pixels, this is the resulting image:

![CLAHE 10 pixels patch](/assets/img/posts/week8/pca_rgb_equalized_10.png)

Observe that the gradients we previously discussed are still absent, but the contrast is more limited. The reason for this is tied to the kernel size. If the kernel size is too big, then CLAHE becomes too close to a global equalization. But if the kernel size is too small, then the windows become so small that the values within them may be too similar, limiting the effectiveness of the equalization.

In conclusion, CLAHE achieves part of the goal of this experiment: with an appropriate kernel size, it can make features in the image more visually distinct. However, we still need to work on removing noise to make the artifacts easier to detect.

### Improved Visibility: CLAHE on ICA's Components

In our last post, we discussed the application of ICA during our pipeline. Through experiments, we concluded it was most effective to use ICA to produce three components from our sample images: paper, text and, noise.

Following that conclusion, this experiment will focus on gauging the improvement in visibility when applying CLAHE to the false-color image produced by assigning each ICA component to a channel, forming an RGB image. In the coming weeks, we'll discuss other ways to combine these components.

By combining the independent components obtained from ICA for the same sample as before, we get the following result:

![ICA to RGB](/assets/img/posts/week8/ica_rgb.png)

If we apply CLAHE to this image, the resulting image is:

![ICA to RGB with CLAHE](/assets/img/posts/week8/ica_rgb_V_equalized.png)

Although the effects of CLAHE on this image are subtle, they are still consistent with the results of our previous experiment: the features of the image became clearer.

We can see this effect more clearly by comparing the ICA components before and after CLAHE. For instance, the text component before CLAHE:

![ICA text](/assets/img/posts/week8/ica_2_V.png)

After CLAHE, it becomes:

![ICA text with CLAHE](/assets/img/posts/week8/ica_2_V_equalized.png)

Observe how the pre-CLAHE version appears blurred compared to the CLAHE-processed version.

Following this logic, when all components are combined after CLAHE, the resulting image is also expected to have more pronounced features.

This experiment indicates that, while not critical, the application of CLAHE can be a helpful post-processing step to better visualize the features of the image.
