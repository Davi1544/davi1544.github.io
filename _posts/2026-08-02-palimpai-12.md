## Experiments

### A Study of LDA

LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis) is a supervised classification algorithm. Our goal with LDA was to classify the pixels of an image in our dataset as overtext, undertext, or parchment. But because LDA is supervised, we first needed some samples of labeled pixels to train the model.

To achieve this, I chose to convert our recurring crop of an MSI cube into an RGB image, and then convert that image into grayscale. Then, because parchment is essentially white and overtext pixels are slightly darker than undertext pixels, I could then separate the three classes based on simple thresholding. In this case, I chose to manually set the thresholds, but Otsu's thresholding also achieved good results. We'll discuss other (probably more accurate) ways to label training pixels in later weeks.

Keep in mind, though, that because we are working with a grayscale image, then the undertext covered by overtext is simply labeled as overtext, so our training data has weak labels.

After training LDA, I applied it to the following patch of a sample image (different from the training image):

![LDA results on B - RGB (%update to spectral later - result is the ~same%)](/assets/img/posts/week12/lda-cropb-rgb.png)

As you can see, the model achieved good results at differentiating the classes: it is able to separate uncovered undertext and overtext. The problem is the model seems to not be able to find the covered undertext. While this issue might be a result of weak labels during training, we must also consider the fact that, because we can't know which pixels of the overtext hide text underneath, we currently have no reliable way to label those pixels for training.

What we can do, though, is examine the distance between each pixel and its average class representative. The idea is distant pixels in the overtext class are probably more likely to contain text underneath. In the following image, the color of a pixel is associated with its Euclidean distance to its average class representative.

![LDA distance mean](/assets/img/posts/week12/lda-crob-distance-mean.png)

Observe that the overtext pixels that are farther from their average representative form a pattern much more aligned with noise (such as mold) than a pattern of possible text underneath. One possible explanation is that, because a large portion of the overtext pixels may contain some text underneath, the average overtext pixel itself may also contain some residual undertext spectral information. So, when measuring the Euclidean distance between overtext pixels and the average representative, then both types of overtext are at a similar distance.

Interestingly, careful examination of this map around visible undertext shows some slight increase in distance where parts of those letters should be.

Another important consideration around this experiment is that we are assuming training data is representative of the testing data, but we had not previously verified this. To test this, I applied the same thresholds used to create the training labels to the testing data and compared the average spectral representatives of each class across the two images. These are the results:

![distance means](/assets/img/posts/week12/crop-a-crop-b-my-labels-spectral-stability.png)

As we can see, the spectrums are consistent. This result shows reflectances values for undertext and overtext are relatively consistent across the whole image. Similar results were achieved when comparing different images.

Finally, I inspected the feature space of the LDA model. The goal was to examine how LDA classifies the pixels of different classes. Keep in mind, though, that the features have no easily interpretable meaning. The resulting feature maps follows.

![LDA Feature map](/assets/img/posts/week12/lda-feature-space.png)

Observe that the classes do overlap, but form fairly concentrated clusters.

## Next Steps

Although LDA did not manage to recover the covered text, it was able to learn to identify overtext with surprisingly high consistency despite the simplicity of its training. 

Because the covered text lies beneath the pixels classified as overtext, we can now focus our analysis specifically on the overtext component. Therefore, in the following posts, I'll focus my efforts on exploring the overtext class, with the immediate goal to determine what remains of the undertext in the spectral data.
