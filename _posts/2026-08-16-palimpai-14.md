# Week 14 - Literature to the Rescue

## Overview

I spent Week 14 reading papers. My main goal was to understand how previous works have used MSI to recover graphite undertext covered by pen ink.

Unfortunately, finding papers addressing this specific problem was difficult, and I didn't manage to find one that fit our problem particularly well, so I decided to read papers on similar problems and general considerations about MSI.

## Articles

### New Frontiers in the Digital Restoration of Hidden Texts in Manuscripts: A Review of the Technical Approaches

The article covers many imaging techniques for recovery of hidden texts, including MSI and HSI (Hyperspectral imaging).

The text focuses more on the differences between MSI and HSI and how both techniques work mechanically.

My main conclusion from this paper is that it reinforces a conclusion we've previously discussed: sometimes, spectral information alone may not be sufficient to recover the undertext. Therefore, in these cases, generative techniques (such as GANs), can be applied.

As discussed in the previous weeks, we have not yet concluded purely spectral separation isn't possible for our problem.

### Fragments under the Lens: A Case Study of Multispectral versus Hyperspectral Imaging for Manuscript Recovery

The paper discusses the differences between MSI and HSI in detail through examples.

The most important differences between MSI and HSI found by the paper are:
1. MSI generally boasts a higher signal-to-noise ratio compared to HSI, making it more adequate to deal with small spectral differences between overtext and undertext;
2. HSI has lower spatial resolution, which makes it harder to identify and read lost texts in HSI cubes;
3. Because HSI has more channels, it is much better suited for identifying the materials used on the manuscript.

As the paper concludes, MSI is generally more suited for palimpsest recovery, while HSI is more adequate for material identification.

My main takeaway from this article is that the relatively small number of channels in our dataset is probably not an inherent obstacle to recovering the lost text.

### Obliterated-writing decipherment using an infrared hyperspectral imaging system

The paper discusses the recovery of obliterated text using HSI. In this case, both undertext and overtext are made using different pen inks. The authors test different types of undertext ink using small HSI cubes in which an obliterating ink has been written over portions of the undertext. The HSI cube is then processed using only PCA.

The main takeaway from this article is that infrared HSI can be a key technique in recovery of the undertext.

In our case, the MSI images we have range from 300 nm to around 950 nm, while the relevant infrared wavelengths used in this paper extend beyond our current spectral range, reaching above 1400 nm. This means we would need new data to apply the techniques used in this paper.

Anyhow, I did speak to my advisor and asked if we could acquire some infrared data from some images in our dataset. I'll give updates on this request in the following weeks.

### Sketch Layer Separation in Multi-Spectral Historical Document Images

The paper describes a technique to recover sketches of historical images using MSI (within roughly the same 400 nm to 1000 nm range as our data). The processing pipeline involves dimensionality reduction followed by clustering. The goal is that sketch pixels would be grouped together.

The pipeline worked seemingly really well in the context of the paper. The problem is, as described in the paper itself, the sketch remained partially visible through the paint. Furthermore, the experiments were conducted on a synthetic example, so "ground truth" was known.

In the case of our problem, the undertext is completely covered in most areas and we have no access to the ground truth. Therefore, it's hard to draw immediate applications of the methods of this paper to our problem.

## Next Steps

Over the next weeks, we'll continue exploring the possible directions discussed in Week 13, while investigating whether the current spectral data contains enough information to distinguish covered undertext from pure strike-through.
