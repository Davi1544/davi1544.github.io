# Strike-through Exploratory Analysis (part 2)

## Overview
Following our experiments from Week 13, I spent this week conducting some additional exploratory experiments on a labeled set of text pixels containing both pure strike-through and covered text pixels. The goal was to continue looking for ways to recover lost text from our multispectral data.

## Experiments

### Dimensionality Reduction: MNF

The first experiment I tried this week involved the pipeline we discussed up to Week 9. I wanted to test the use of the MNF (Minimum Noise Fraction) algorithm instead of PCA. MNF reduces the number of channels in an image based on an estimate of noise. I wanted to test this algorithm because we have already established that there is little spectral difference between pure strike-through pixels and covered text pixels in our dataset.

The following images show the first three components identified by MNF for a sample image:

![MNF 1](/assets/img/posts/week16/img_4_front_mnf_1.png)

![MNF 2](/assets/img/posts/week16/img_4_front_mnf_2.png)

![MNF 3](/assets/img/posts/week16/img_4_front_mnf_3.png)

As you can see, these components are not easily interpretable and do not successfully recover the crossed-out text. However, in the third component, some portions of the strike-through pixels are darker, especially in areas that are more likely to contain covered text.

While MNF did not recover the lost text, it did produce results that PCA did not.

### Non-linear Recovery: SVM

In Week 13, we discussed how there is some spectral difference between pure strike-through pixels and covered text pixels in our dataset, but most of the techniques we have tried to separate them were linear.

There are multiple non-linear techniques worth trying, but I decided to try SVM this week because we can easily vary the kernel it uses. Additionally, because we concluded that channels 11, 12, and 13 (starting from 0) are the main channels separating the strike-through pixels, I decided to also vary the pixel representations used to train the SVM.

I ran three main experiments with SVM. Unfortunately, varying the kernel function did not meaningfully affect any of these experiments.

In the first experiment, I represented strike-through pixels using their values on channels 11 and 13. By training the SVM on labeled data, we get the following separation:

![SVM 11/13](/assets/img/posts/week16/svm_11_13.png)

Observe that the model converges on essentially labeling every pixel as pure strike-through, despite the stratification of the training dataset and the model's average accuracy of 90%.

Next, I decided to simply include all channels, even though I did not expect this to result in a better model. The resulting separation follows:

![SVM all](/assets/img/posts/week16/svm_all.png)

As expected, the separation is not much better, despite the average accuracy of 95%.

Finally, I decided to train the model on a single number per pixel: the sum of its channel responses. I decided to try this because, as we concluded in previous posts, for every channel, the average response of strike-through pixels that contain lost text is higher than the average response of those that do not. Additionally, the variance is not too high in either set. Therefore, I concluded that the sum of the channel responses could be a reasonable indicator of each pixel's class.

The separation follows:

![SVM sum](/assets/img/posts/week16/svm_sum.png)

As with the previous experiments, the separation is not sufficient, despite the average accuracy of 85%.

In summary, SVM did not manage to recover the lost text, despite achieving relatively high accuracies in all experiments.
