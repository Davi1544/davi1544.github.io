# Why Latent Diffusion?

## Overview

This week I revisited *High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models* (Rombach et al., CVPR 2022), the paper behind Stable Diffusion. I had read it a while ago, but since I've started a Deep Learning project and plan on using Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs), I wanted to reread it with the project in mind.

This post covers what the paper proposes, and, more importantly, which of its ideas matter for our project.

## The Paper in Short

Diffusion Models (DMs) generate images by learning to reverse a fixed noising process, denoising step by step. They work well in both conditional and unconditional settings, but they operate directly on pixels, so training is expensive, and even inference requires hundreds to thousands of forward passes through a large network to produce a single image.

The paper's main idea is to split the problem in two:

1. **Perceptual compression.** First, train an autoencoder that maps an image of size $H \times W \times 3$ to a smaller latent $h \times w \times c$. The autoencoder is trained with a perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial objective, which prevents the blurry reconstructions that a perceptual loss alone would produce. The latent is also regularized, so it doesn't drift to a high-variance space, which is harder to decode.
2. **Generative modeling in latent space.** Then, instead of learning to denoise images, the diffusion model learns to denoise the latents $z$. During inference, we sample a latent from noise, denoise it, and pass the result through the decoder to get back to pixel space.

The motivation is that natural images contain a lot of detail that is imperceptible to humans, but a pixel-space model still spends capacity and processing time on it. The autoencoder removes that detail up front, so the diffusion model can focus on the semantically meaningful structure, which also accelerates inference.

Two other ingredients matter for us. First, the denoiser is a UNet, which has a strong inductive bias for spatially structured data (convolutions, up/downsampling, skip connections, multi-scale features). Since the latent keeps its two spatial dimensions, that bias is preserved. Second, the paper adds conditioning: instead of learning $p(z)$, the model learns $p(z \mid y)$, where $y$ can be a text prompt, a guide image, a mask, etc. 

## How This Connects to Our Project

### The input to Stable Diffusion Inpainting matches the paper's conditioning recipe

The checkpoint we plan to use compresses $512 \times 512$ images by a factor of 8 with a VAE, and its UNet takes 9 input channels: 4 for the noisy latent, 4 for the latent of the masked image, and 1 for the mask. This is the paper's conditioning-by-concatenation idea applied to inpainting: the masked region (the strikethrough) is what the model must fill in, and the rest of the image guides it.

In our case, the "image" is not RGB. It is a 3-channel reduction of 16 spectral bands (PCA, MNF, or a shallow convolutional autoencoder). 

### The autoencoder is the part I'm most worried about

The paper's premise is that the autoencoder discards imperceptible detail while preserving what matters. But that notion of "what matters" was learned on natural images. Our targets are thin, low-contrast pencil strokes under ink, which look a lot like the kind of fine detail a perceptual autoencoder might smooth away, especially at $f = 8$.

This is why the October plan includes an explicit check of the VAE: before any fine-tuning, we need to know how well it reconstructs our reduced multispectral images, including handwriting, in regions with and without strikethrough. If the VAE can't represent the strokes, no amount of LoRA fine-tuning on the UNet will recover them, since the UNet only ever sees latents.

### Learning a distribution is both a feature and a risk

Since the model learns a distribution over latents, not a single answer, sampling the same masked region multiple times gives different reconstructions. We intend to use that as a rough measure of uncertainty: regions where samples agree are more trustworthy, and regions where they diverge indicate ambiguity.

The flip side is hallucination. The model is trained to produce plausible images, and plausibility is not actual evidence. That is why the final output has to be treated as a *reading hypothesis* to be checked against the spectral data, not as a reconstruction of what was actually written.

## Open Questions

- How much stroke detail does the Stable Diffusion VAE preserve in our (reduced) multispectral images?
- Does the UNet's inductive bias for spatial structure help with handwriting strokes?
- The cross-attention pathway is designed for text conditioning. For us it will be mostly unused, so all the information has to enter through the concatenated channels. Is that enough?
