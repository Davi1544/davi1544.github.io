# Week 21 - Exploring Layer Separation Models

## Overview

This week, I spent some time reading two papers on the separation of overlapping text in historical manuscripts. Both approaches model the observed pixels as a mixture of different sources (close modelling to Spectral Unmixing), such as the recto, verso, and the manuscript itself.

Although these papers primarily address bleed-through removal, I believe their underlying ideas could also be useful for our palimpsest recovery problem.

## Articles

### A Non-Stationary Density Model to Separate Overlapped Texts in Degraded Documents

The paper was written by Anna Tonazzini, Pasquale Savino, and Emanuele Salerno.

The authors propose a model that represents the observed manuscript images as mixtures of two underlying text layers: recto and verso. The model accounts for the interference between these layers through pixel-dependent coefficients, essentially making so it learns how to separate actual text from bleed-through in overlapping regions.

An interesting aspect of this paper is that the authors explicitly discuss both recto-verso interference removal and palimpsest recovery. In the last case, they consider two multispectral channels as separate observations of the same manuscript, with different contributions from the two text layers.

### Training a Shallow NN to Erase Ink Seepage in Historical Manuscripts Based on a Degradation Model

The paper was written by Pasquale Savino and Anna Tonazzini.

The authors propose a shallow neural network that classifies pixels into foreground text, bleed-through, and background. 

The network is trained to recognize the different contributions to each pixel, using information from both sides of the manuscript. The authors also discuss the difficulty of dealing with overlapping text, where the contributions of the two layers are not easy to distinguish.

## Potential Applications to PalimpAI

I believe the underlying idea behind these papers could be useful for our problem.

One possibility would be to assume that two channels in our multispectral cube represent different mixtures of the original manuscript and the strike-through. In this case, we could model each observed pixel as a combination of the underlying text and the strike-through.

An interesting advantage of this approach is that we would not need to deal with the image alignment problems associated with recto-verso pairs. Since both observations would come from the same multispectral cube, their pixels would already be spatially aligned.

However, there is an important limitation. Both papers rely on some degree of difference between the appearance of the foreground text and the bleed-through. This difference is what allows their models to estimate the contribution of each layer (e.g, in the first paper, the occlusion limit is set to 0.9, essentially meaning the model can't separate the two texts if their colors are too close).

Unfortunately, as we have discussed in previous weeks, our dataset does not exhibit a meaningful spectral difference between pure strike-through pixels and covered undertext pixels in any spectral channel. Therefore, these methods cannot be directly applied to our **current** data.

Even still, I believe this is an interesting direction to keep in mind. If we manage to obtain data with more distinguishable spectral responses, or find a way to introduce additional information into the model, these approaches could become useful for our problem.
